Private Networks and Industrial Infrastructure: A Capital Allocation Lens

Chris Upstone October 2026 6 min read

The convergence of operational technology, IT, and wireless connectivity is reshaping how resources businesses manage risk and create long-term value. For executives running capital-intensive operations, private networks are becoming the backbone of autonomous production — and the decision about how to deploy, operate, and fund them is an operational decision with long-term consequences.

Why this is an infrastructure question, not a IT question

Private wireless networks — private 4G and 5G owned and operated by the enterprise, not the carrier — are not a new IT line item. They are industrial infrastructure. In mining, oil and gas, and heavy manufacturing, they are becoming the connectivity backbone that enables autonomous operations, remote control of equipment, real-time safety systems, and the integration of AI into physical operations.

The proof is in the field. Consider a large Pilbara iron ore operation running 50-plus ultra-class haul trucks across an open-pit network. Autonomous haulage systems — trucks that navigate, load, haul, and dump without a driver in the cab — have moved from trial to production at scale. The results are well-documented: truck utilisation rises 15-20% because autonomous trucks do not take shift breaks, meal breaks, or fatigue management stops. Cycle times become tighter and more predictable because every truck drives to the same speed profile, brakes identically, and follows the same haul line. Fuel consumption drops 10-15%. Haul-truck incidents fall 40-90% in reported cases. One Pilbara-scale operation has reported savings of approximately $200 million per year once autonomous haulage was fully operational across its network.

But the haul trucks are only the visible layer. The same private network that connects the trucks enables autonomous drill rigs — which prepare blast patterns with precision that improves fragmentation, shovel productivity, and crushing performance downstream. Autonomous drilling can increase productivity by up to 60%, and it keeps operators out of the blast zone. Electric shovels are remotely controlled from a control centre 1,000+ kilometres away. Collision avoidance systems, connected worker monitoring, and environmental sensors all run on the same network. The private wireless network is not connecting a truck. It is connecting an entire autonomous operating system — and when any part of that network drops, the production line stops with it.

This is not a pilot. It is a production network underpinning a production decision about how a multi-billion-dollar asset is operated. When connectivity fails on a Tier 1 iron ore mine, the cost is not lost email. It is lost production — measured in thousands of dollars per minute across a fleet moving 300+ million tonnes a year.

The market is scaling — and shifting to managed services

Global spending on private mobile networks is projected to grow from $1.2 billion in 2024 to $21 billion by 2030 — a 62% compound annual growth rate. By 2030, Juniper Research estimates the mining industry alone will operate 1,704 private networks worldwide. Manufacturing has led adoption, but utilities, extractive industries, and logistics are now scaling fastest.

The delivery model is shifting in parallel. Enterprises are moving away from buying hardware and operating it themselves, toward managed service contracts where a provider designs, builds, operates, and evolves the network under a subscription or per-site fee. This is the same transition that enterprise IT went through with cloud — capex to opex, owned infrastructure to contracted service. It matters because it changes who carries the risk, who owns the capability, and what the long-term cost actually looks like.

The capital allocation lens

The question is not whether to deploy private networks — the operational case is increasingly clear. The question is how to think about them as assets.

Private networks share characteristics with traditional infrastructure: long useful lives (10-15 years for radio hardware, longer for fibre and core), high upfront capital, and criticality to the operations they enable. They are not easily replaced — once a network is embedded in an autonomous mining operation, switching costs are extreme. The return is not a standalone cash flow. It is the avoided cost of production downtime, the enabled value of automation, and the reduced risk of safety incidents. This makes them more like a toll road inside a business than an IT system — the return is in what it enables, not what it charges.

For operators delivering these networks as managed services, the economics are different and more conventional: long-term contracts, recurring revenue, high switching costs, and operational scale across multiple sites. The private 5G service market is projected to reach $40 billion by 2034, growing at 22% annually. Telcos, systems integrators, and pure-play providers are all positioning. The winners will be those who can deliver across multiple sites, multiple industries, and multiple technology generations — because the hardest part of this business is not the first deployment, it is the tenth.

What executives should be deciding

Is this a network or a capability? If the private network enables autonomous operations, remote control, or AI-driven decision-making, it is not IT infrastructure. It is operational infrastructure. The funding, staffing, and risk lens should match — which means the COO and the operations leadership should own the decision, not the CIO.

Who operates the network, and what does the contract actually guarantee? Most managed service SLAs are written around availability and incident response, not around performance against a use case. If the network is up but the autonomous trucks are running at half speed because throughput is degraded, the SLA is met and the production loss is yours. Executives should ensure the contract measures what the operation actually depends on.

What is the total cost of ownership over the asset life? The build cost is the headline. The operating cost — managed services, internal specialist headcount, spectrum, upgrades, and the inevitable re-planning as the site evolves — is the number that determines whether the business case holds. A network that costs $1 million to build and $200,000 per year to operate is a $2 million asset over a decade, not a $1 million one.

The operator's decision

Private networks are operational infrastructure, not an IT line item, and the decision about how to deploy, operate, and fund them sits with operations leadership. The hardest part is not the first deployment — it is the tenth, and the hundredth, across sites, industries, and technology generations. The business case lives or dies on the operating cost, the contract structure, and whether the SLA measures what production actually depends on.

For the operator, the question is whether the network is treated as the production-critical asset it has become — funded, governed, and contracted accordingly. A private network that enables autonomous haulage, remote control, and AI-driven operations is not connecting a truck. It is underwriting the operating model. The decision deserves the rigour that implies.
