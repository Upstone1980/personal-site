Grid Investment and the Governance of Long-Duration Capital

Chris Upstone October 2026 6 min read

Australia's energy transition is one of the largest infrastructure programmes in the country's history. For boards, it is not a question of whether to participate. It is a question of whether the board understands the risk it is carrying, whether the capital decisions under its oversight account for transition exposure, and whether the governance framework is equal to a 25-year cycle.

The scale of the build — and what boards need to see in it

AEMO's 2026 Integrated System Plan outlines the numbers: $106 billion in capital investment by 2050 to transform the National Electricity Market. The plan calls for 120 GW of grid-scale wind and solar, 50 GW of utility-scale storage and hydro, 17 GW of flexible gas, and 6,000 kilometres of new transmission lines. The system moves from 21 GW of coal today to a diversified portfolio that is fundamentally different in structure, location, and operating characteristics.

For a board, the detail that matters is not the megawatts. It is that the system the business depends on is being rebuilt while it is still operating — and the replacement is arriving on a schedule the board does not control. The transmission component — 6,000 km of new lines — is only 7% of the total capital cost, but it is the 7% that unlocks the other 93%. AEMO estimates the transmission build delivers $28 billion in net market benefits. Without it, the renewable generation and storage cannot reach the demand centres where 80% of Australia's population lives. Transmission is the gating dependency, and dependencies are a governance matter.

The constraint is not capital. It is delivery.

More than $60 billion of power-sector investment is expected between 2026 and 2030. The money is available. What is not available is enough of what the build requires: time, equipment, and people. Only 2,800 km of the required transmission is underway. The remaining 3,200 km depends on regulatory determinations, community engagement, environmental approvals, and capital mobilisation. High-voltage substation equipment from Korean and European manufacturers now carries 36-48 month lead times — up from 12-18 months pre-2020 — because US hyperscaler demand has captured the priority queue. HumeLink, a critical 500kV Snowy 2.0 transmission link, has seen its cost escalate from $3.3 billion to nearly $5 billion with an 18-24 month schedule slip. Construction cost inflation runs 18-22% above 2020 baselines.

Coal is leaving on its own clock. Eraring, the country's largest coal plant, has been extended twice and is now scheduled to close in April 2029. Yallourn closes in June 2028. Returns on the $60 billion in new investment depend on whether replacement generation, firming, and transmission arrive before coal leaves. The sequencing — not the capital — is the risk. This is the scenario the risk committee should be stress-testing: what happens to the business if replacement capacity slips another 18 months?

The allocation gap as a governance question

The most striking number in the transition is what is missing. Australia's superannuation funds hold $4.5 trillion in retirement savings — more than the entire Canadian pension system. Yet the top 30 funds have directly invested just $771 million in Australian renewable energy projects since 2020 — 0.8% of the $99 billion deployed. Canadian pension funds have invested more in Australian renewables than Australian super funds. The capital is there. The allocation is not.

The reasons are structural. Super funds are familiar with toll roads, airports, and ports — well-understood, clearly regulated assets with decades of performance data. Renewable energy infrastructure is younger, the policy environment has been less stable, and the YFS performance test can penalise funds for holding long-duration assets that do not track listed benchmarks over short windows. The result is a market where foreign capital is funding Australia's energy transition, and Australian retirement savings are largely watching from the sidelines.

For a director of a super fund, this is not an investment opportunity to consider. It is a governance question. If members are retiring into an economy being rebuilt around decarbonised energy, and the fund's portfolio is structurally underexposed to the assets building that economy, is the investment mandate keeping pace with the transition the members will live through? The fiduciary duty is not to chase yield. It is to ensure the portfolio reflects the world members are retiring into — and the YFS performance test, by penalising long-duration holdings over short windows, may be creating a structural misalignment between the regulator's measurement framework and the members' actual horizon. A board that has not examined that misalignment has not fully discharged its duty.

Regulatory exposure and the cost of policy instability

Transmission assets carry a regulated WACC of 3% — lower than generation because the risk is lower. The AER's allowed return for the 2025-30 regulatory period sits at 5.8-6.4% real post-tax, against Australian 10-year bond yields at 4.25-4.50%. That is a structural 150-200 basis point gap between the regulatory allowed return and the market-required return — and it is constraining private investment in regulated transmission.

For boards, regulatory risk in the energy transition operates on two levels. The first is direct: if your business is a regulated network, the allowed return determines whether capital can be raised. The 150-200 basis point gap is not an academic observation — it is the difference between a buildable project and one that does not clear its cost of capital. The second is indirect: if the transition's delivery depends on regulated transmission being built, and the regulatory framework is not delivering returns that attract capital, the transition slips. Policy instability compounds both. A board that does not understand the regulatory architecture governing its energy exposure — the AER's determinations, the YFS test, the policy cycle — is not exercising full oversight.

What boards should be asking

What is the board's line of sight on transition risk? Energy costs, grid connection timelines, and carbon exposure are now material to investment decisions across mining, manufacturing, telecommunications, and data centres. If the board cannot state, in one sentence, where the business sits on the transition's cost curve and delivery timeline, the risk register is incomplete.

Has the board stress-tested the sequencing? The transition's risk is not whether capital is available — it is whether replacement capacity arrives before coal leaves. Boards should require management to present scenarios for 12, 18, and 24-month delivery slippage, and to show what each means for energy cost, operational continuity, and capital commitment.

Is the capital allocation framework transition-aware? Whether the business is investing in transition assets directly, or depending on transition infrastructure being built by others, the board's capital approval framework should account for transition exposure as a cost, a risk, and a contingency — not treat it as a sustainability-report footnote.

What is the regulatory exposure, and who is watching it? The AER's allowed return, the YFS performance test, and the policy cycle each carry the power to change the economics of a transition investment between approval and delivery. Boards should satisfy themselves that regulatory monitoring is assigned, resourced, and reported — not absorbed into general management updates.

The governance obligation

For boards, the energy transition is not an investment decision to be made. It is a governance obligation to be discharged. The system the business depends on is being rebuilt. The capital is available but the delivery is uncertain. The regulatory framework is still finding its footing. And the institutional capital closest to home — Australian superannuation — is structurally under-allocated to the assets building the next economy, in part because the measurement framework discourages exactly the long-duration holdings the transition requires.

The board that treats the energy transition as a sustainability report has misread it. The board that treats it as a capital allocation and risk oversight question — with scenarios, regulatory monitoring, and a clear line of sight on transition exposure — is doing the job the next 25 years will require.
