AI, OT Cyber Security and the Boardroom Governance Imperative

Chris Upstone October 2026 6 min read

The air gap is gone. AI is weaponising the attack surface faster than most boards can monitor it. In capital-intensive industries, the consequences of an OT cyber incident are not measured in data loss but in production shutdowns, safety events, and environmental damage. Most boards are not structured to govern this risk. They need to be.

The threat has changed

For decades, operational technology, the systems that control physical processes in mining, oil and gas, manufacturing, and utilities, sat behind an air gap. OT networks were isolated from corporate IT and the internet. Security meant physical access control, not firewalls.

That world no longer exists. Autonomous haul trucks communicate over private 4G and 5G networks. Grid operators monitor SCADA systems that control power flow to millions of customers. Water utilities manage treatment plants and distribution networks through connected PLCs. Manufacturers run production lines where robotic systems, sensors, and industrial control systems are linked to enterprise networks and cloud platforms. Predictive maintenance platforms pull real-time sensor data into cloud analytics. Remote operations centres control equipment thousands of kilometres away. AI systems trained on operational data make decisions that directly affect physical processes. The IT/OT convergence that enables these capabilities has dissolved the air gap and created an attack surface that traditional industrial security was never designed to defend.

AI is amplifying both sides of the equation. On the defensive side, AI-powered behavioural analytics can detect anomalous commands across industrial networks in sub-millisecond timeframes, compressing incident detection from weeks to days. On the offensive side, AI is a far more dangerous accelerant. AI models can now autonomously discover zero-day vulnerabilities across operating systems with a 72% exploit success rate. Time-to-exploit has collapsed from 2.3 years in 2018 to under one day in 2026. AI is generating attack paths in OT environments that, in the words of one industry expert, are "borderline beyond our typical comprehension." The threat is no longer a lone attacker with a laptop. It is an AI system that can map your control loops, exfiltrate your configuration files, and learn how your physical processes operate well enough to disrupt them.

Why OT is different

When a bank's AI model makes a poor credit decision, the consequence is a bad loan. When a grid operator's SCADA system is compromised, the consequence can be a regional blackout affecting hospitals, water treatment plants, and emergency services. When an autonomous haul truck's control system is attacked, the consequence can be a collision involving a 300-tonne vehicle with no operator in the cab to intervene. When a manufacturing plant's PLCs are overridden, the consequence can be a production line running beyond safe pressure limits. The stakes in OT cyber are qualitatively different from enterprise IT, and most board governance frameworks have not caught up.

The numbers are moving fast. Industrial organisations experienced over 2,000 ransomware attacks in a 12-month period, more than any other sector, every single month. The industrial sector accounted for nearly 30% of all ransomware activity, with capital goods manufacturers, machinery, and construction and engineering among the most targeted sub-sectors. A single OT breach in a heavy industry operation now averages $14.2 million when production downtime, equipment damage, and regulatory penalties are factored in. And the broadest-reaching cyber events are third-party breaches of shared software platforms that entire industries depend on. When a major ERP provider to the resources sector was compromised, the operational impact rippled across multiple companies because the systems that manage diesel deliveries, chemical supplies, and production scheduling went down with it. When a widely used IT management platform was breached, it opened a backdoor into thousands of organisations across energy, manufacturing, and utilities simultaneously. Your exposure is not just your own systems. It is the shared infrastructure your industry runs on.

This is the governance challenge. The risk lives in your vendor ecosystem, your supply chain, and the interconnected operational platforms your production depends on. A board that limits its cyber oversight to "are we secure?" is asking the wrong question. The right question is whether the board understands the full extent of its exposure, and whether that exposure is being governed at the level it demands.

The governance gap

In most boards, cyber risk oversight sits with the audit committee. This is a legacy of the days when cyber was an IT compliance issue: data privacy, breach notification, regulatory reporting. The audit committee was the natural home because the risk was framed in financial and compliance terms.

That framing is no longer adequate. When a cyber incident can shut down a power station, halt a production line, contaminate a water supply, or trigger an environmental event, the risk has moved from a compliance category to a strategic and operational one. The NACD's 2026 Director's Handbook on Cyber-Risk Oversight is explicit: "Cybersecurity has evolved from a peripheral technology issue into a central pillar of corporate governance and a core element of fulfilling the board's fiduciary duties." The World Economic Forum identifies AI as the most significant driver of changes in cybersecurity. Yet only 40% of Fortune 100 companies have assigned AI oversight to a board-level committee, and most of those have parked it with the audit committee, where the disclosures tend to be thinner than when oversight sits with a dedicated technology or risk committee.

The audit committee is already carrying financial reporting, internal controls, external audit, and regulatory compliance. Adding AI-enabled OT cyber risk to that agenda is overload. The consequence is that cyber risk gets briefed but not governed. The board hears a CISO presentation, notes the threat landscape, and moves to the next agenda item. Scenarios go untested. Capital allocation goes unchallenged. Escalation protocols go unrehearsed. The minutes reflect a briefing, not deliberation, and under the Delaware Caremark line of cases, that distinction matters for director liability.

The case for a dedicated AI and Cyber Security sub-committee

Boards in capital-intensive industries should establish a dedicated AI and Cyber Security sub-committee, or at minimum, a formalised cyber oversight structure with a defined charter, meeting cadence, and escalation protocol that operates independently of the audit committee's compliance agenda.

The sub-committee should have three core responsibilities. First, strategic risk oversight: understanding the company's cyber risk profile as a business continuity and safety issue, with a focus on OT environments where the consequences are physical. Second, AI governance: overseeing how AI is deployed in the business, both the operational AI that controls physical equipment and the enterprise AI that employees are already using, often without oversight. Third, third-party and supply chain risk: ensuring the cyber posture of critical vendors, equipment manufacturers, and technology providers is assessed, monitored, and contracted for. The broadest cyber events in heavy industry have come through third parties, not direct attacks.

The sub-committee needs the right composition. This does not mean every director must be a cyber expert. 86% of Fortune 100 companies now cite cybersecurity as an area of expertise they seek on the board, but expertise at the board level means the ability to ask the right questions and challenge management, not to configure a firewall. The sub-committee should have access to independent expertise, external advisors who can provide technical and strategic evaluations of the company's posture, threat modelling, and incident response readiness. The NACD is clear: even with qualified in-house staff, "cyber risk evolves faster than many organisations can track internally."

What the sub-committee should be asking

Do we have visibility over our OT environment? Industrial organisations with comprehensive OT visibility detect and contain ransomware in an average of five days, compared to the industry-wide average of 42 days. If the board cannot get a straight answer to "how many OT assets do we have, and which are exposed," the oversight gap starts there.

How is AI changing our risk profile, on both sides? AI is simultaneously improving defensive capabilities and accelerating offensive capabilities. The sub-committee should require management to report on how AI is being used defensively in the business, how AI is changing the threat landscape the business faces, and what the gap looks like between the two.

What is our third-party exposure, and who owns it? The broadest cyber events in heavy industry have come through shared software platforms and third-party providers, not direct attacks. The sub-committee should understand which vendors and suppliers represent single points of failure across the operation, and whether those relationships are governed by contracts that include cyber security standards, incident notification obligations, and the right to audit.

Have we stress-tested our incident response? Over half of Fortune 100 companies now disclose that their preparedness efforts include simulations and tabletop exercises. If the sub-committee has not run a scenario where an OT breach shuts down production for 11 days, which happened to a major resources operation in 2024, costing an estimated $180 million in lost output, or a scenario where a utility's grid management system is locked by ransomware during a summer demand peak, the board is governing on assumption, not evidence.

The governance obligation

The boards that treat cyber as an IT compliance issue, briefed quarterly by the CISO, noted in the minutes, filed under the audit committee, are governing the risk the way it looked five years ago. AI has collapsed the time between vulnerability and exploit. OT convergence has made the consequences physical. The regulatory net is tightening. The EU's NIS2 Directive classifies energy, mining, manufacturing, and utilities as critical infrastructure with 24-hour incident reporting and prescriptive OT security standards, and Australian obligations under the SOCI Act are moving in the same direction.

A dedicated AI and Cyber Security sub-committee is the structural change that ensures the board has the focus, the expertise, and the time to govern a risk that is now central to the safety, continuity, and value of the business. The boards that make this change will not eliminate cyber risk. No governance structure can. But they will be the ones who can demonstrate, in the aftermath of an incident, that they oversaw the risk with the seriousness and structure it demanded. That is what fiduciary duty looks like in the age of AI-enabled OT risk.
